Material honesty in practice

There is a chair in the corner of Jun Takahashi's studio that has been there for eleven years. It is not a prototype. It is not a showpiece. It is simply the chair he sits in when he needs to think. The wood has darkened where his hands rest. The seat has worn smooth.

This is what material honesty looks like when it is not a marketing term. The chair was made from a single piece of walnut, shaped by hand over three weeks. No filler, no veneer, no stain. Just wood and time and the accumulated patina of use.

A room for thinking

The studio faces east. Takahashi works only in natural light, which means his hours shift with the seasons. In winter he begins at seven and stops at four. In summer he starts at six and works until seven. The rhythm is not romantic. It is practical. Artificial light, he says, hides the truth of a surface.

The walls are bare concrete. The floor is poured resin. There are no shelves of reference books, no mood boards, no samples pinned to the wall. The only decoration is a single ikebana arrangement that changes weekly. Everything else is either a tool or a project in progress.
